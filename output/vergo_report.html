How to read this report

RULA, or Rapid Upper Limb Assessment, is a screening tool used to assess postural exposure in the neck, trunk, upper arms, lower arms, wrists, muscle use, force/load, and activity. The score helps identify whether further ergonomic investigation or task changes may be needed. RULA is not an injury prediction tool. It should be interpreted together with the video context, task frequency, duration, force, workstation layout, and worker feedback.

Section 3 – Task-Based Risk Exposure Analysis

Wrist Deviation and Extreme Wrist Posture

Across all sampled frames, both wrists are consistently observed in extreme positions, with wrist angles elevated. This posture places the wrists in significant deviation from neutral, increasing the risk of cumulative strain and discomfort over time. The exposure is driven by the mechanics of the task, which appear to require sustained or repetitive wrist extension or deviation. This risk is modifiable through changes in task setup, tool design, or work method, and addressing it could help reduce the likelihood of upper limb discomfort or injury.

Neck Flexion and Head Position

The neck is frequently observed in a flexed or extended posture, with several frames showing high neck angles well above the optimal range. This suggests that the worker is either looking down or up for extended periods, likely due to the placement of the work or the need to visually inspect items. Neck flexion is a recognized risk factor for musculoskeletal discomfort, particularly when sustained or repeated. While some neck movement may be inherent to the task, optimizing work height and visual access could help reduce this exposure.

Lower Arm and Elbow Angle

Lower arm postures vary between low and high exposure levels, with several frames showing elbow angles outside the optimal range. This pattern indicates that the worker's forearms are not consistently supported or positioned within the recommended ergonomic zone. The exposure is likely driven by the reach distance and the orientation of the task surface. Adjustments to work height, reach, or the use of supports could help bring the lower arm posture closer to neutral and reduce cumulative loading.

Trunk and Upper Arm Posture

Trunk and upper arm postures are generally within the low exposure range, with minor deviations observed in some frames. The trunk remains relatively upright, and upper arms are not excessively elevated or abducted. These findings suggest that the task does not impose significant trunk or shoulder loading, and these regions are not primary concerns for this task. Maintaining these positive aspects should be a focus in any future task redesign.

Section 4 – Overall Observations

The main ergonomic drivers in this task are the sustained wrist deviation and non-neutral neck postures, both of which are consistent across the sampled frames. These exposures are likely influenced by the height and orientation of the work surface, as well as the need for visual inspection or manual manipulation.

Positive aspects include generally neutral trunk and upper arm postures, indicating that the task setup does not require excessive reaching or trunk flexion. However, the cumulative effect of repetitive wrist and neck positions warrants review, particularly if the task is performed frequently or for extended periods.

Section 5 – Overall Recommendations

Adjust Work Height and Orientation

Consider raising or angling the work surface to bring items closer to elbow height and within the worker's visual field. This change can help reduce both wrist deviation and neck flexion by promoting more neutral postures. Implementation may involve workstation modification or use of adjustable platforms.

Introduce Ergonomic Tools or Supports

Evaluate the use of tools or supports that allow the wrists to remain in a more neutral position during task performance. Ergonomically designed handles or wrist supports can reduce extreme wrist angles and lower the risk of cumulative strain. Selection should be based on the specific task requirements and worker feedback.

Encourage Task Rotation or Micro-Breaks

Implement regular task rotation or scheduled micro-breaks to limit the duration of sustained wrist and neck postures. This approach helps reduce cumulative exposure and allows for recovery, especially in repetitive or high-frequency tasks. Scheduling can be integrated into existing workflow with minimal disruption.

Provide Training on Neutral Posture Techniques

Offer targeted training to workers on maintaining neutral wrist and neck postures during manual tasks. Training can increase awareness of risk factors and promote safer movement patterns. Incorporate practical demonstrations and feedback to reinforce learning and support behavior change.
