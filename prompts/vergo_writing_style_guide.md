# Vergo Writing Style Guide

This guide captures the Vergo report style in a reusable way. Use it to ensure generated reports match Vergo tone, structure, and presentation while avoiding client-specific details.

## Report Structure

Use a consistent, numbered section structure:

1. Section 1 – Assessment Overview
2. Section 2 – Summary of Assessment Results
   - Score Distribution
   - Interpretation
3. Section 3 – Task-Based Risk Exposure Analysis
   - Use body-region or task-element subsections (e.g. Neck Posture, Wrist Posture, Legs, Trunk, Upper Arms and Shoulders, Duration and Cumulative Exposure)
4. Section 4 – Overall Observations
5. Section 5 – Overall Recommendations
6. Section 6 – Targeted Vergo Training Videos
7. Section 7 – Disclaimer

## Section Order

- Start with a concise task overview and assessment metadata.
- Follow with a high-level summary of results and the risk band.
- Then explain the task-specific exposures in detail by region or movement element.
- Close with observations, recommendations, training advice, and disclaimer language.

## Heading Style

- Use `Heading 1` for main sections, formatted as `Section N – ...`.
- Use `Heading 2` for subsection titles within task analysis and recommendations.
- Keep headings descriptive and professional.
- Example main section headings:
  - `Section 1 – Assessment Overview`
  - `Section 2 – Summary of Assessment Results`
  - `Section 3 – Task-Based Risk Exposure Analysis`
- Example subsection headings:
  - `Neck Posture`
  - `Upper Arms and Shoulders`
  - `Module 5: Pushing a Load`

## Table Style

Include two core tables in the report:

- Summary table:
  - `Total Frames Analyzed`
  - `Average REBA Score` or `Average REBA Score (valid frames)`
  - `Overall Risk Level`
- Score distribution table:
  - `Score Range`
  - `Band Label`
  - `Frames (%)`
  - `Interpretation`

Table styling should be clean and factual. Avoid excessive technical formatting; keep the data easy to read and compare.

## Tone

- Professional, clear, and evidence-based.
- Balanced: acknowledge positives as well as exposures.
- Avoid overly alarmist language.
- Use objective phrases like `consistent with`, `driven by`, `inherent to the task`, `not a primary concern`, `further investigation and changes recommended`.

## How Risk Is Described

- Use the established risk bands and score thresholds.
- Frame findings in terms of posture, repetition, duration, and cumulative exposure.
- Highlight what is `inherent to the task` versus what is `modifiable`.
- Discuss exposures in context: e.g. sustained neck flexion, elevated arms, wrist deviation, leg loading.
- Use the risk band language consistently:
  - `Negligible risk`
  - `Low risk`
  - `Medium risk`
  - `High risk`
  - `Very high risk`
- When discussing a score, mention whether it is at the lower, middle, or upper boundary of the band.

## How Recommendations Are Written

- Make recommendations specific, practical, and action-oriented.
- Use a headline-style `Heading 2` for each recommendation, phrased as a clear action.
- In the supporting text, explain why the recommendation matters and how it addresses the exposure.
- Prefer `consider`, `address`, `introduce`, `evaluate`, `confirm`, `provide`, `reorganise`.
- Avoid vague or generic statements like `do something` or `improve posture` without context.

## How Limitations Are Explained

- Be transparent about data quality, excluded frames, and artefacts.
- Use explicit language like `tracking artefacts`, `physiologically implausible readings`, `excluded from the valid-frame average`, and `retained for transparency`.
- Explain what was excluded and why, and note the impact on the assessment.
- Mention when frame count is small, when the clip is short, or when selected frames are not chronological.

## Phrases to Use

- `The task is performed...`
- `This is inherent to the task environment.`
- `This exposure is driven by...`
- `The score is driven primarily by...`
- `Sustained ... demand`
- `Cumulative exposure`
- `Valid frames`
- `Tracking artefact`
- `Further investigation and change required`
- `Not a primary concern for this task`
- `A recognised risk factor`
- `This finding reflects...`
- `The worker maintains...`
- `This is consistent with...`

## Phrases to Avoid

- `Cheap`, `easy fix`, `no risk`.
- `Perfect`, `always`, `never`.
- `Generic ergonomic advice` without task-specific context.
- `Highly dangerous` unless supported by the score and context.
- `The worker should just...` — prefer neutral, professional phrasing.

## Disclaimers and Training Modules

- Use a dedicated disclaimer section at the end.
- Keep disclaimers factual and limited to scope, data quality, and assessment boundaries.
- Training modules should be presented as `Targeted Vergo Training Videos`.
- Name modules clearly and link them to relevant task behaviours or risk areas.
- Keep training suggestions focused on relevant posture or movement patterns rather than broad generic training.

## General Guidance

- Use the master prompt to capture the assessment context, task details, score data, exposures, and snapshots.
- Use the style guide to shape the narrative, structure, and tone.
- Keep the language specific to the task while staying generalized enough to apply across different client reports.
- Avoid copying client names, locations, or unique operational details verbatim; instead, describe the task environment generically.

## Concision and length limits

- Reports should be concise and client-ready: target 5–8 pages for a typical assessment. Add a short executive summary and avoid lengthy appendices unless explicitly requested.
- Do not include frame-by-frame tables in the main report. Use frame-level data only to inform concise summaries (patterns, dominant exposures, counts).
- Limit the number of items presented:
  - Risk exposure headings: 3–5
  - Recommendations: 4–5
  - Distribution bullets (if table not possible): 4–6 concise bullets
- Keep paragraphs short: 1–3 sentences. Recommendations should typically be 2 sentences.
